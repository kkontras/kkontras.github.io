# From Tempi in Greece to Mexico’s Madres Buscadoras

Crime born of state corruption knows no borders. Yet it shares a global and common architecture of cover up. On the occasion of the anniversary of the Tempi tragedy and the anger over the absence of justice in Greece, it is worth turning our gaze toward Mexico. Not to compare magnitudes but to understand how impunity becomes a regime. A place where institutional decay does not erase only evidence and responsibility, but also people. This distant perspective may help us realize that our reaction is the only barrier, both for ourselves and as an example for a world that needs it.

Returning from a trip to Mexico, along with a few small souvenirs, I also brought back a harsh realization, simple for many but difficult to digest. Crimes involving the state are everywhere products of the same corruption and share a common pattern.

When we arrived it did not take long for the tourist façade to crack and reveal a more truthful picture. In the very first days we met a group of travelers who told us a story from the previous night. One of those stories that in Greece would sound like a movie script. They had gone out in Mexico City and were drinking beers while moving from one bar to another. Drinking in the street is normally forbidden. The police approached them and the familiar intimidation began. Under the pretext of being taken to the station and given a fine of 2000 pesos, around €250, the officers forced them into the patrol car. The ride never ended at the police station. Instead a strange safari through the city’s ATMs began. The officers forced the group to withdraw the maximum amount possible from their cards, while the fine kept increasing and eventually reached €1,500. When their cards were finally blocked, the police threw them out of the patrol car and continued their shift undisturbed.

We encountered a similar situation on the Pacific beaches. As soon as night fell police officers on ATVs swept across the sand looking for those who remained on the beach under the pretext of suppressing drugs. They would stop anyone taking an evening walk and search them thoroughly. If they found drugs, which incidentally were being loudly advertised just two streets above, they simply took their payment and continued on their way without confiscating anything. If they found nothing rumors said they had ways of producing them themselves in order to secure their share.

Another image we kept came from the city of Oaxaca. Later we realized that these scenes repeat themselves across the country. The first was police pickup trucks circulating among the crowd carrying in the back a heavily armed sniper standing upright and ready for action. I remember watching the vehicle disappear and wondering, who is he aiming at and who is he protecting?

The second image was in the city’s central square. It is a city whose center gives the impression of a wealthy and well ordered country, clean, illuminated, calm and safe. Yet right in the middle of the square some banners and a large notice board told the truth of the place. The headline read “¡Buscando a Nuestros Familiares Desaparecidos!”, meaning “Searching for our disappeared relatives.” Beneath the title were fifty or sixty photographs of people who one day simply vanished. Nearby a relatively new banner told the same story about a specific person who disappeared in August 2021. Almost five years later. Do you understand it? Do you understand the strength it takes to keep searching?

Later we arrived in a town in the mountains near San Cristóbal. There are people there who study the situation and analyze it almost scientifically, with the kind of distance we often maintain in Europe. The soul may be absent but the numbers speak. Since 2007 approximately 130,000 disappearances have been officially recorded and it is estimated that only two out of ten cases are reported. The rest search, struggle and suffer in silence. Every day 35 disappearances are officially reported while the real number is estimated to be around 175. Every single day.

---

To assign responsibility for these acts we must listen to the story of the parastate. Armed organizations can broadly be divided into two categories. The first are the cartels. In this country drug traffickers are not merely businessmen. They possess organized or semi organized armies. The second category consists of paramilitary organizations, often funded and trained by the state even though they theoretically operate independently. These groups often share targets with the cartels, including people who oppose state development projects or demand justice, for example Samir Flores Soberanes.

These armies have no limits and no respect for human rights. Crimes are committed openly in the streets and in broad daylight. A characteristic example is the events of February 22, 2026 in the state of Jalisco, where a wave of arson attacks erupted after the army struck one of the cartels.

The war against drugs and violence is convenient. Every new government proclaims a new beginning and creates military groups that supposedly confront the problem. At the end of each administration the baton is simply passed on. Soldiers who destroyed the previous cartel often become leaders of the next one. This is said to have happened with Los Zetas and later with the Jalisco New Generation Cartel. In my eyes cartel and state play the same game. Different roles, different profits, the same game.

> “Yet there are those who ruined their recipe, descendants of Zapata in the land of the Mexicans.”[1]

This is how people write about the rebels. In solidarity with them stands a group called the Madres Buscadoras, meaning the searching mothers ([movndmx.org](https://movndmx.org)). When the state conducts superficial investigations these women take matters into their own hands. Families searching for the disappeared recently discovered improvised crematoriums at a ranch in Guadalajara together with more than 400 pairs of shoes and human remains.

Does this seem extreme and distant? It horrifies me. Yet I find a similarity with our own tragedy in Tempi, where soil was removed from the crash site and dumped elsewhere. State corruption hides the same story. A different scale, a different world, the same roles and the same motives.

In Mexico those who oppose these interests are often killed. In Greece we are not so brutal, but we rely on something else. Public opinion eventually forgets. The word responsibility is what disturbs us the most. In both countries politicians helped shape the problem. Their inaction is obvious. That is why they play the same role in the story whether we speak of 400, 57 or thousands more across the world. There is however one difference that makes Greece a bright example in this struggle. The families of the Tempi victims demand justice and impartial trials. In Mexico many families only hope to find their loved ones, even if they are dead. In Greece we can do more. We can show the world that the guilty can be punished and that the motives of power and profit can be exposed. Your anger and your contribution to uncovering the truth can give hope to people across the world.

From Mexico to Greece, for our people and for theirs, for a world beautiful, just and equal for all,
