\title{The Metabolic Mind: Reclaiming the Conditions of Intelligence}

The question of intelligence has in recent years occupied a central place across a wide range of contexts, from philosophical reflection and scientific inquiry to corporate strategy and governmental policy. Yet despite its ubiquity, the question itself does not admit of an immediate or unified answer. From a philosophical perspective, this is not a defect but a revealing feature: the concept of intelligence gathers together a constellation of more fundamental concerns that resist straightforward resolution. To ask what intelligence is or whether it can be grasped, understood, measured, or even meaningfully said to exist, is already to presuppose answers to deeper questions about thought, understanding, and the conditions under which a world can be encountered as intelligible at all.

When pursued with sufficient rigor, questions about intelligence thus lead beyond observable performances and capacities to the underlying problem of mind. Any attempt to characterize intelligence, whether in human or artificial systems, draws upon concepts that have been debated throughout the history of philosophy: representation, intentionality, consciousness, rationality, and understanding. These concepts do not merely describe empirical regularities but articulate competing accounts of what it is for a system to think or to mean at all. As a result, discussions of intelligence repeatedly confront a tension between, on the one hand, explanations framed in terms of physical mechanisms, causal processes, or functional organization, and, on the other hand, accounts that appeal to subjective experience, semantic content, or first-person access. This tension is not accidental. It reflects the philosophical difficulty concerning of how mental phenomena are related to the material conditions that appear to sustain them, a difficulty that has structured debates about the nature of mind from early western philosophy through contemporary cognitive science \parencite{aristoteles1931anima, descartes2008meditations, fodor1974special}. To ask whether intelligence can be instantiated, reproduced, or recognized is therefore inseparable from the question of how mental life itself is situated within the natural world.


\subsection*{The Cartesian Legacy and the Mind-Body Problem}

The Cartesian conception of mind as a thinking, non-extended entity immediately raises a fundamental difficulty: how such a domain could relate to the physical world at all. If mental life is essentially different in kind from bodily processes, it becomes unclear how thoughts and experiences could give rise to action, or how physical events could be accompanied by conscious awareness. This formulation, establishes the canonical form of the mind-body problem and continues to shape contemporary debates, even where its metaphysical commitments are explicitly rejected \parencite{descartes2008meditations}.


More recent attempts to account for the mind aim to avoid classical substance dualism while still doing justice to what seems distinctive about mental life. These approaches accept that mental processes are physically realized, but resist the idea that they can be fully understood using only the concepts of matter, mechanism, and causal interaction. The motivation for this resistance lies not in a commitment to non-physical entities, but in a perceived mismatch between how mental phenomena are experienced and how physical systems are typically described \parencite{chalmers1997conscious}.

Physical systems are ordinarily characterized in terms of parts, spatial extension, and public observability: they can be divided, measured, and described from a third-person point of view. By contrast, mental experiences present themselves as unified, non-spatial, and accessible only to the subject who has them. A sensation or a thought does not seem to consist of separable components in the way a physical object does, nor does it appear to occupy a determinate region of space. Because of this difference, it is difficult to see how a mental state could be straightforwardly identified with a physical state without remainder.

These considerations do not establish that minds are non-physical. Rather, they clarify why many philosophers remain skeptical of purely reductive accounts of mentality. Even if every mental event depends on physical processes, the concepts used to describe physical systems may fail to capture features of experience that are essential to understanding what mental life is like. In this sense, the problem is not that physical explanations are false, but that they may be incomplete.

\subsection*{Conceivability, Perspective, and the Explanatory Gap}

A further pressure on physicalist accounts arises not from structural comparison but from reflection on possibility. It appears at least conceivable that all the physical facts about a system could be fixed while the character of experience differs, or is absent altogether. Contemporary versions of this line of reasoning suggest that physical description, however complete, may fail to determine the subjective character of mental life \parencite{kripke1980naming}. Whether such conceivability reflects a genuine metaphysical distinction or merely a limitation of our concepts remains disputed, but it sharpens the sense that there is a gap between describing a system’s physical organization and accounting for what it is like to be that system.

This gap is given particularly clear expression in the claim that conscious experience is inseparable from a point of view. Even a complete account of an organism’s physical structure and functional organization may leave unanswered the question of what it is like to be that organism \parencite{nagel2024like}. The difficulty at issue is not merely that some information is missing, but that the form of explanation itself is misaligned with its target. Descriptions that aim for objectivity and generality proceed by abstracting away from perspective, whereas conscious experience is essentially perspectival. The resulting tension is therefore not one that can be resolved by adding further detail to third-person accounts, but reflects a deeper mismatch between explanatory frameworks.

\subsection*{Functionalism and Its Limits}

Rather than treating this tension as evidence for a separate, non-physical domain, many philosophers have sought to reconceptualize mental phenomena in terms of the roles they play within a system. On this approach, what makes a state mental is not its intrinsic qualitative character but the pattern of causal relations it bears to sensory inputs, behavioral outputs, and other internal states. By shifting attention from inner substance to functional organization, this strategy aims to preserve the explanatory power of the natural sciences while remaining neutral about the specific material realization of mental states \parencite{Putnam1960-PUTMAM}. In doing so, it offers a way of addressing the explanatory gap without abandoning a broadly naturalistic framework.


Yet this functional reorientation does not fully dissolve the difficulty. Critics have argued that characterizing mental states solely in terms of what they do risks leaving aside precisely what motivated the problem in the first place: the qualitative character of experience itself~\parencite{nagel2024like}. Explaining how a system processes information or produces intelligent behavior may succeed at the level of prediction and control, while leaving unanswered the question of why such processes should be accompanied by experience at all.

This worry is often illustrated by the well-known thought experiment involving Mary, introduced by Frank Jackson. Mary is imagined as a scientist who knows all the physical and functional facts about human color vision but has lived her entire life in a black-and-white environment. When she first sees color, it seems that she learns something new, the what it is like to see red, even though no new physical information is acquired \parencite{jackson1998epiphenomenal}. The intuitive force of the example lies in the suggestion that complete physical or functional knowledge may still leave out an aspect of experience itself. Whether or not one accepts Jackson’s conclusions, the case brings into focus the idea that experience has a qualitative dimension that resists purely functional description.

A closely related concern is sharpened by the Chinese Room thought experiment. John Searle invites us to imagine a system that manipulates symbols according to formal rules in such a way that it produces behavior indistinguishable from genuine understanding, despite lacking any comprehension of the symbols it processes \parencite{searle1980minds}. The force of the example lies in the distinction it draws between syntactic manipulation and semantic understanding. Even if a system instantiates the relevant functional organization, Searle argues, this alone does not guarantee that it understands what it is doing. Taken together, these thought experiments underscore a persistent worry: explanations that succeed in accounting for intelligent performance may nevertheless fall short of explaining what it is like to understand or experience anything at all.


\subsection*{Mind as Biological Metabolism}

An intervention in debates about mind and consciousness argues that the apparent intractability of the so-called hard problem is exacerbated by an impoverished conception of living matter \parencite{godfrey2016mind}. From this perspective, functionalist approaches that treat mental states as largely independent of their physical realization are misleading, insofar as they portray minds as abstract patterns detachable from the material processes that sustain them. By contrast, mental phenomena are understood as deeply constrained by the specific forms of physical organization characteristic of living systems. On this view, the emergence and development of subjectivity are not incidental byproducts of information processing, but arise from the requirements imposed by life itself.

Central to this position is a reconceptualization of biological matter. Living systems are not merely collections of components obeying mechanical laws, but dynamically organized, high-energy structures that persist far from thermodynamic equilibrium. In this sense, what distinguishes biological existence is not simply that it is alive, but that it must actively maintain itself against entropic decay. This continual work of self-preservation introduces a distinctive mode of being: the organism must regulate its internal processes, defend its organization, and remain responsive to conditions that threaten or support its continued existence. It is this active resistance to equilibrium, rather than the presence of a passive experiential substrate, that grounds the emergence of a point of view.

From this perspective, subjectivity is not something that merely happens to a system, but is inseparable from the system’s ongoing self-sustaining activity. The organism does not simply undergo states; it exists in a condition where its own persistence is at stake. This stake confers significance on the world and structures experience in terms of relevance, urgency, and concern. Subjectivity, in this sense, is the inward aspect of a system whose material organization requires continuous regulation and adaptive engagement with its environment.

Seen in this light, the mind-body gap is not bridged by adding consciousness as an extra layer atop physical processes, but by refining our understanding of what kinds of physical processes living matter instantiates. When matter is understood in its bio-logical sense, the transition from physical organization to lived experience appears less mysterious. If subjectivity is functionally tied to recursive, self-maintaining biological activity, then the mind is not separate from the body but is the experiential expression of life’s distinctive mode of physical organization.


\subsection*{From Viability to Norms}

If subjectivity is grounded in the self-maintaining organization of living matter, the next step is to clarify how such organization gives rise to a normative relation between organism and world. One line of thought~\parencite{jonas2001phenomenon} emphasizes that life differs from inert matter not merely in complexity but in its mode of being. A living system must continually produce and preserve itself; its existence is something it has to achieve rather than something that simply happens to it. In this sense, life introduces a fundamental distinction between continuation and breakdown, between maintaining form and losing it altogether. This distinction marks the first emergence of value in nature: to be alive is already to be exposed to success and failure in a way that non-living systems are not.


This insight can be sharpened by introducing the notion of viability. For a living system, there are specific ranges of internal states and environmental relations under which it can persist, and outside of which it deteriorates or dies. Viability, however, is not yet normativity. It becomes normatively significant only because the organism must actively regulate itself in order to remain within those bounds. Unlike a machine that merely functions or malfunctions relative to an external purpose, the organism’s regulatory activity is directed toward its own continued existence. As ~\cite{thompson2010mind} emphasizes, this self-directed regulation transforms mere physical constraints into standards that matter for the system itself.

Once viability is enacted through ongoing regulation, the environment can no longer be understood as a neutral field of causes. Features of the world acquire significance relative to the organism’s needs and vulnerabilities: some conditions support continued existence, others threaten it, and still others demand compensatory responses. This selective sensitivity is what Thompson describes as "sense-making". The organism does not merely register stimuli; it encounters a world structured in terms of relevance and irrelevance, benefit and danger. Importantly, this structure does not presuppose explicit goals, representations, or judgments. It arises directly from the organism’s embodied effort to remain viable.

At this point, normativity can be clearly distinguished from both mere physical causation and explicit rule-following. Norms are not imposed from outside, nor are they consciously represented from within. They are implicit in the organism’s way of being: to act well or poorly is to act in a way that sustains or undermines the conditions of one’s own existence. Normativity is intrinsic to autonomous biological systems because their activity is continuously evaluated by its consequences for viability  \parencite{di2005autopoiesis}, viability that can be translated in many forms. What matters, in the most basic sense, is not defined by abstract criteria but by the organism’s ongoing struggle to persist.

This conception of normativity provides the missing link between metabolism and intelligence. Metabolic necessity explains why a living system must regulate itself; normativity explains how the world comes to be encountered in terms of better and worse relative to that necessity. Intelligence, as the following section will argue, consists in the increasing flexibility, sensitivity, and scope with which a system navigates this norm-structured world. Rather than standing opposed to biological life, intelligence emerges as one of its most elaborated expressions.

If normativity articulates the space in which a living system encounters its world as a matter of concern, intelligence can be understood as a distinctive way of operating within that space. Metabolic necessity explains why regulation is required at all and provides the means to ground the properties of the mind.  Normativity explains how regulation acquires the character of success and failure relative to the system itself. Intelligence enters when this norm-governed engagement becomes increasingly flexible, context-sensitive, and extensible. An intelligent system is not merely one that maintains viability, but one that can modulate how it does so across varying conditions.

This conception distinguishes intelligence from both brute persistence and mechanical control. A system may remain viable through rigid or narrowly tuned regulatory mechanisms without thereby exhibiting intelligence. Intelligence, by contrast, involves the capacity to cope with novelty, which non-biological intelligence fails to do so far \parencite{he2025academic}, to reconfigure strategies in response to changing circumstances, and to integrate multiple normative constraints at once. What marks intelligence is not the presence of norms as such, but the ability to navigate a norm-structured environment in ways that are adaptive without being pre-scripted. In this sense, intelligence reflects a deepening of agency rather than a departure from biological regulation.

\subsection*{Intelligence as Norm-Sensitive Engagement}

Importantly, this account allows for degrees and kinds of intelligence without collapsing them into a single metric. As living systems increase in organizational complexity, the normative spaces they inhabit expand accordingly. What counts as acting well is no longer exhausted by immediate metabolic preservation, but can involve longer-term coordination, social interaction, and participation in shared practices. Human intelligence, on this view, does not transcend biological normativity but elaborates it: linguistic reasoning, artistic creation, and scientific inquiry are higher-order ways of acting appropriately within complex normative domains that presuppose more basic forms of embodied sense-making.

This perspective also clarifies why intelligence cannot be fully captured by descriptions of performance alone. To behave successfully is not yet to act intelligently unless that behavior is responsive to what matters for the system in question. A purely formal system may generate correct outputs without inhabiting a normative perspective at all, whereas even modest organisms exhibit forms of intelligence precisely because their actions are constrained and guided by what is at stake for them. Intelligence, therefore, is inseparable from the standpoint of a system for which things can go better or worse, and cannot be understood independently of the normative conditions that give rise to that standpoint.

Seen in this light, intelligence is neither an abstract computational property nor an optional addition to life. It is a mode of engagement that emerges when biological normativity is taken up and extended through increasingly sophisticated forms of regulation and agency. Rather than standing opposed to metabolism, intelligence appears as one of its most refined expressions: the capacity of a living system to navigate necessity not merely by persisting, but by doing so well.
